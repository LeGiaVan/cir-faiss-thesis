\begin{abstract}
Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) systems answer queries formed by a reference image and a free-text modifier. Current state-of-the-art models, including CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, and Pic2Word, report retrieval accuracy based entirely on exhaustive (brute-force) search. While this exact search is feasible for small laboratory datasets, it becomes increasingly impractical in real-world applications with massive image galleries, where scanning every image challenges real-time latency requirements. To address this gap, this paper observes what happens when exact search is replaced by Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) indexing from the FAISS library (IVFFlat, HNSWFlat, IVFPQ) across three CIR models and three benchmarks of increasing gallery scale. We first reproduce the published validation-set results, uncovering and fixing significant reproducibility bugs (such as feature-normalization mismatches and batch-indexing errors) before any ANN experiment could be considered valid. We then adopt the standard ANN recall metric and adapt it as \emph{Recall Retention} for CIR evaluation, measuring an ANN index's neighbor-set overlap with exact search directly, decoupling indexing error from a model's inherent retrieval quality, and sweep various index configurations with gallery-size-adaptive clustering parameters. Observations from the benchmark show that the relationship between accuracy and speed is strongly scale-dependent: HNSWFlat demonstrates significant speedups with minimal accuracy drop on large galleries, but its advantage shrinks on smaller datasets, while IVFFlat can occasionally be slower than brute-force search at small scales; IVFPQ, meanwhile, exhibits extreme speedups and memory reduction alongside severe accuracy degradation, which we trace to reconstruction error vastly outscaling the score gap needed for correct ranking. All latency and accuracy figures are averaged over repeated trials to guard against measurement noise. These findings highlight that the effectiveness of ANN indexing in CIR is not uniform, but a scale- and index-type-dependent observation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) is the task of retrieving an image from a gallery given a query composed of a reference image and a natural-language modifier describing a desired change (for example, a photograph of a black leather jacket paired with the instruction ``the same but change material to suede, color to green.'') The task is central to e-commerce visual search, where users routinely wish to refine a search result relative to a product they have already found. Since 2023, three models have substantially advanced CIR accuracy by building on the joint image-text embedding space of CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}: CLIP4Cir~\cite{baldrati2023composed}, a supervised approach that fuses image and text features through a trained Combiner network; and SEARLE~\cite{baldrati2023zero} and Pic2Word~\cite{saito2023pic2word}, two zero-shot approaches that map the reference image into a pseudo-word token consumed by the frozen CLIP text encoder.

All three papers evaluate retrieval quality by computing the cosine similarity between the query vector and \emph{every} gallery vector, $O(N)$ brute-force search. On the galleries used for evaluation (a few thousand images for FashionIQ and CIRR), this is inexpensive: our own brute-force measurements average well under a millisecond per query. On CIRCO, whose gallery already contains 123,403 images, brute-force search already costs on the order of 1--3~ms per query in our measurements (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and a production catalog with millions of listings would push this well past what a real-time service can afford. The standard industrial remedy is Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search, implemented in libraries such as FAISS~\cite{johnson2019faiss}, which trades a controlled amount of accuracy for sub-linear query cost. Yet none of the three CIR papers above, nor any published CIR work to our knowledge, reports what happens to retrieval quality once exact search is replaced by an ANN index. The trade-off is assumed, not measured.

This gap motivates the following research question: \emph{does replacing exact search with FAISS-based approximate nearest-neighbor indexing preserve Composed Image Retrieval quality while reducing query cost, and how do these behaviors depend on gallery scale and on the CIR model family (supervised vs.\ zero-shot)?}

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a single reproducible evaluation harness (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) around a common BaseAdapter interface that wraps CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, and Pic2Word, each evaluated under its dataset-specific protocol.
    \item In the course of validating this harness against the published validation-set numbers, we uncover and fix reproducibility bugs that would otherwise have invalidated every downstream ANN experiment, reporting this diagnostic process as a methodological contribution.
    \item We adopt the standard ANN recall metric~\cite{aumuller2017annbenchmarks} and adapt it as \emph{Recall Retention} for CIR evaluation. This metric measures the neighbor-set overlap between an ANN index and exact search directly, isolating the system-level indexing error from the CIR model's inherent prediction error, which is a critical decoupling that raw Recall@K cannot provide.
    \item We sweep various FAISS index configurations across multiple (model, dataset) combinations with a gallery-size-adaptive parameter grid, and observe that the resulting accuracy, speed, and memory behaviors depend strongly on gallery scale and on the index family, yielding concrete deployment observations rather than a single blanket recommendation.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork} reviews CIR models and ANN search algorithms. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the evaluation harness, the per-dataset protocol, and the Recall Retention metric. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the reproducibility validation, the sweep results, and discusses the three main findings. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\subsection{Composed Image Retrieval Models}
\label{subsec:cir-models}

CLIP4Cir~\cite{baldrati2023composed} fine-tunes a CLIP backbone (ResNet-50x4) and trains a lightweight Combiner network that fuses the reference-image and text-modifier embeddings through a learned dynamic scalar gate, producing a single query vector. It is evaluated on FashionIQ~\cite{wu2021fashioniq} and CIRR~\cite{liu2021cirr} in a fully supervised setting, i.e., trained end-to-end on each dataset's triplets.

SEARLE~\cite{baldrati2023zero} instead targets the zero-shot setting: a small network $\Phi$ maps the reference image into a pseudo-word token $S{*}$, which is spliced into the modifier text (``a photo of $S{*}$ that {\dots}'') and encoded by an otherwise frozen CLIP text encoder. Because it requires no CIR-specific training data, SEARLE generalizes to open-domain galleries, and its authors introduce CIRCO, a 123K-image benchmark built on the COCO2017 unlabeled pool, specifically to evaluate this generalization at scale.

Pic2Word~\cite{saito2023pic2word} follows the same pseudo-word-token philosophy as SEARLE but replaces the textual-inversion network with a simpler MLP (IM2TEXT) trained on large-scale image-caption pairs. It is evaluated on the same three benchmarks and serves in this paper as a second, architecturally independent zero-shot model against which SEARLE's behavior can be cross-checked.

\subsection{Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search}
\label{subsec:ann}

FAISS~\cite{johnson2019faiss} provides several families of ANN index relevant to this study. IndexIVFFlat~\cite{sivic2003ivf} partitions the vector space into nlist Voronoi cells via $k$-means and restricts search to the nprobe nearest cells. IndexHNSWFlat~\cite{malkov2018hnsw} builds a multi-layer navigable small-world graph and performs greedy graph traversal, controlled by graph degree $M$ and search-time beam width efSearch. IndexIVFPQ~\cite{jegou2011pq} combines IVF partitioning with Product Quantization, compressing each vector into $m$ sub-quantized codes to reduce memory footprint at the cost of reconstruction error. All three are approximations of IndexFlatIP, the exhaustive exact-search baseline used (implicitly) by the CIR papers reviewed above.

\subsection{Research Gap Summary}
\label{subsec:research-gap}

The CIR models reviewed in Section~\ref{subsec:cir-models} optimize exclusively at the \emph{model layer} (i.e., how to produce the most accurate query vector) and evaluate that vector with an $O(N)$ exhaustive comparison against the gallery. Current literature treats CIR evaluation monolithically, grouping algorithmic prediction errors and system-level approximation errors into a single Recall@K score. 

None examines the \emph{index layer}: how retrieval quality degrades, and by how much query cost falls, when exact search is replaced by one of the ANN structures described in Section~\ref{subsec:ann}. This is precisely the research gap this paper addresses (Gap 2 in our proposal). By separating the model's inherent accuracy from the index's approximation loss via Recall Retention, we provide a system-level analysis jointly across a supervised model (CLIP4Cir) and two independently architected zero-shot models (SEARLE, Pic2Word), spanning a 50$\times$ range of gallery scale.

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{subsec:architecture}

All three CIR models, despite unrelated training procedures, reduce at inference time to the same operation: produce one $d$-dimensional, $L_2$-normalized vector per query, to be compared against $d$-dimensional gallery vectors by cosine similarity ($d=640$ for CLIP4Cir, $512$ for SEARLE, $768$ for Pic2Word). We exploit this to define a single BaseAdapter interface with two core methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item extract\_image\_features(image): encodes a raw gallery image into a normalized vector.
    \item extract\_query\_features(reference\_image, text\_modifier): fuses a reference image and a text modifier into a query vector.
\end{itemize}
This interface is implemented once per model, and a single evaluation harness (FaissEvaluator) consumes the resulting vectors regardless of which model produced them. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, the pipeline is split into two phases: gallery images are embedded once and indexed (Phase 1), after which arbitrary queries are embedded and searched against that index (Phase 2). This separation lets the (expensive, one-off) embedding step be fully decoupled from the (repeated) FAISS benchmarking step.

\subsection{FAISS Index Configurations and Adaptive Parameter Sweep}
\label{subsec:sweep}

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} lists the index types and parameter grid swept for every (model, dataset) combination. A first implementation used a fixed nlist across all datasets; this triggered FAISS's own ``too few points per centroid'' warning on the smaller galleries (CIRR, FashionIQ) and produced artificially collapsed IVFFlat/IVFPQ recall as a result. FAISS's official guidance~\cite{johnson2019faiss,faisswiki2024guidelines} recommends $nlist \in [4\sqrt{N}, 16\sqrt{N}]$ together with a training-data floor of roughly $30 \times nlist$ vectors per index (i.e., $N \gtrsim 30\,nlist$ for stable $k$-means centroids). Applying the guideline's own lower bound, $nlist = 4\sqrt{N}$, to our two smaller-scale benchmarks violates this floor: for CIRR ($N{=}2{,}297$) it requires $N \geq 5{,}751$ training vectors while only $2{,}297$ exist, and the shortfall is similar for every FashionIQ category ($N{=}3{,}817$--$6{,}346$, requiring $7{,}414$--$9{,}559$). Only on CIRCO ($N{=}123{,}403$) does $4\sqrt{N}=1{,}405$ comfortably clear its own floor ($42{,}155 \leq 123{,}403$). We therefore extend the swept grid one step below the official range, to $nlist_{\text{small}} \approx \sqrt{N}$ (which satisfies the $30\times$ floor on every benchmark tested, including CIRR and FashionIQ) while retaining $nlist_{\text{large}} \approx 4\sqrt{N}$, the official range's own lower bound, for IVFFlat; IVFPQ uses $nlist_{\text{small}}$ throughout, since its already-small per-cell vector budget (after Product Quantization) makes the more conservative choice necessary for stable cluster training. In short, the adaptive formula is not an arbitrary interpolation but the range of nlist values that keeps every one of our galleries above FAISS's own minimum-training-data guideline, which the fixed-nlist baseline and even the official range's lower bound fail to do at CIR-typical gallery scales.

\subsection{Recall Retention Metric}
\label{subsec:retention}

The Recall@K and mAP@K metrics used by the source papers compare retrieved results against ground truth, conflating two distinct sources of error: the model's own retrieval quality, and the approximation error introduced by the index. To isolate the latter, we define Recall Retention@K as the mean neighbor-set overlap between an ANN index's top-$K$ result and exact search's top-$K$ result for the same query, independent of whether either set contains the ground-truth target:
\begin{equation}
\text{RecallRetention@}K = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{\left| \text{top-}K_{\text{ANN}}(q) \cap \text{top-}K_{\text{Flat}}(q) \right|}{K}
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}$ is the query set and $\text{top-}K_{\text{Flat}}(q)$ is always computed first and cached as the baseline for every subsequent ANN configuration on the same (model, dataset) pair. This is the standard notion of ``recall'' in the ANN-benchmarking literature and should not be confused with Information-Retrieval-style Recall@K reported elsewhere in this paper.

\subsection{Repeated-Measurement Protocol}
\label{subsec:repeated}

A single run of any benchmark conflates the quantity of interest with measurement noise, such as system jitter for latency and run-to-run variation for indices with randomized construction procedures. Before designing the repeated-measurement protocol, we first tested empirically, rather than assumed, which of the four index types actually exhibit construction randomness: IndexFlatIP, IndexIVFFlat, and IndexIVFPQ were rebuilt from the same vectors ten times each and found to be bit-identical across rebuilds (FAISS's k-means training uses a fixed default seed), whereas IndexHNSWFlat rebuilds differed by approximately $0.5\%$ of search results, traceable to the random level assignment step in HNSW graph construction. This finding fixes the design of the protocol: \emph{latency and QPS} are measured with 1 warm-up query batch followed by $N=7$ timed search trials on a single fixed index, for every index type, since search-time system noise affects all of them equally; \emph{Recall Retention and speedup}, by contrast, are only re-measured across independent rebuilds for HNSWFlat (using 5 independent graph constructions, each searched 3 times) since the other three index types are deterministic and a second rebuild would reproduce identical numbers. All HNSWFlat figures reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are therefore mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 5 builds; figures for FlatIP, IVFFlat, and IVFPQ are single point estimates with empirically-justified zero variance across rebuilds (search-level timing noise is still averaged over 7 trials for all four).

\subsection{Sanity-Check Verification Protocol}
\label{subsec:sanitycheck}

Before any ANN experiment is run, brute-force (IndexFlatIP) Recall@K/mAP@K for every (model, dataset) pair is compared against the corresponding published numbers, with a tolerance of roughly 1--2 percentage points. This tolerance margin accounts for hardware-level and software-level variations, such as differences in GPU architectures, PyTorch/FAISS versions, and floating-point rounding arithmetic (e.g., FP16 vs. FP32) between our local environment and the original authors' environments. 

This step is treated as a hard gate: if the exact-search baseline itself does not match the published protocol, no conclusion drawn from an approximate index built on top of it can be trusted. Section~\ref{subsec:sanitycheckresults} reports the outcome of this gate, including two significant bugs it surfaced. It also details the distinction between direct validation-set comparisons and indirect test-set comparisons.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:implementation}

All experiments were conducted on an Intel Core i7-14700 processor (20 cores / 28 threads, base clock 2.1 GHz) with 16\,GB of RAM. FAISS index construction and search utilized OpenMP multi-threading configured to the default thread count (all 28 hardware threads). Query encoding and search were processed using a batch size of 256. No deliberate cache warming was performed between configurations; each index was searched from a cold start after construction. To minimize system measurement noise, all latency and throughput figures are averaged over 7 repeated search trials (following a warm-up batch), as specified in Section~\ref{subsec:repeated}.

For the efficiency metrics reported in Table~\ref{tab:sweetspot} and the subsequent discussion, we use the following definitions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speedup}: The ratio of brute-force exact search latency to ANN search latency ($\text{latency}_{\text{FlatIP}} / \text{latency}_{\text{ANN}}$) over the exact same query set, calculated as a per-query average.
    \item \textbf{Memory ratio (Mem.\ ratio)}: The serialized size of the ANN index divided by the serialized size of the exact FlatIP index. Both IndexHNSWFlat and IndexIVFFlat ratios naturally exceed $100\%$ because they store the full-precision gallery vectors alongside their required navigational structures (the HNSW graph and IVF lists, respectively). By contrast, IndexIVFPQ discards the original vectors in favor of compact quantized codes, yielding ratios far below $100\%$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sanity-Check: Validation Against Published Results}
\label{subsec:sanitycheckresults}

Applying the protocol of Section~\ref{subsec:sanitycheck} initially surfaced a severe anomaly: CLIP4Cir on CIRR scored Recall@1~$=0.81\%$, far below even the weakest configuration reported in the original paper ($21.38\%$). Diagnosis proceeded by elimination: the checkpoint's SHA-256 hash matched a fresh download from the authors' Google Drive folder (ruling out a corrupted file), and a targeted script revealed that $98.4\%$ of queries retrieved \emph{the reference image itself} at rank 1 instead of the target. Inspecting the official CLIP4Cir training and validation code showed that the Combiner network is trained and evaluated with the image feature $L_2$-normalized but the text feature left \emph{unnormalized}; our adapter normalized both, distorting the learned residual gate $output = \text{MLP}(\cdot) + \text{gate}\cdot\text{text} + (1-\text{gate})\cdot\text{image}$ enough that the image term dominated almost every query. Removing the erroneous text normalization dropped the self-retrieval rate to $7.5\%$ and raised Recall@1 to $37.38\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:sanitycheck}), within $2.5$--$5.8$ points of the published (RN50, a smaller backbone than ours) validation numbers. Additionally, a second bug caused Pic2Word's query encoder to crash immediately during batched inference because it called a tensor-indexing routine designed only for single-sample inputs. This was fixed by switching to a batch-compatible pooling routine used in the authors' own evaluation script.

The initial CLIP4Cir/FashionIQ gap ($\approx\!11$ points) was ultimately traced back to the same normalization bug: the original Combiner network expects completely unnormalized vectors for both image and text. Removing the $L_2$-normalization from the image feature extraction restored FashionIQ Recall@10 to 43.04\%, perfectly matching the published 43.34\% validation score for the RN50x4 backbone. With this final fix, all nine (model, dataset) cells meet or nearly meet the sanity-check tolerance; two zero-shot models (SEARLE and Pic2Word) independently matched CIRCO within $\leq\!1$ mAP point of each other's deviation from the published table, providing cross-model evidence that the evaluation harness itself, as opposed to any single checkpoint, is implemented correctly.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\label{subsec:resultsdiscussion}
Table~\ref{tab:sweetspot} reports, for every (dataset, model, index type), the highest-speedup configuration that retains at least $95\%$ Recall Retention@10 (or, where no configuration reaches that bar, the best Recall Retention@10 actually observed, marked ``No'').

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{Finding 1: HNSWFlat is the safest choice, and its speed advantage over brute force widens predictably with gallery scale.}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/fig_crossover.pdf}
\vspace{-0.2cm}
\caption{Latency vs.\ gallery size $N$ for SEARLE (log-log axes). Markers are the eight \emph{measured} points, where HNSWFlat is faster at every one. Lines are the fitted models of Eq.~\eqref{eq:crossover}; they cross near $N{\approx}4{,}300$, a small-sample fitting artifact. No measured point ever shows FlatIP faster.}
\label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

On CIRCO (123K images), HNSWFlat reaches a $22.8\times$ speedup (SEARLE) at $97.0\%\pm0.3\%$ Recall Retention@10 (mean $\pm$ std, 5 independent graph builds; Section~\ref{subsec:repeated}). Table~\ref{tab:accuracy_impact} further shows that for HNSWFlat and IVFFlat, the end-to-end metric loss ($\Delta$Metric) is consistently within 1--2 points. On CIRR and FashionIQ (2.3K--6.3K images), the best achievable speedup at the same retention bar collapses to $1$--$5\times$; re-examining the full 120-configuration sweep shows every configuration with speedup $<1\times$ is an IVFFlat configuration, and no HNSWFlat configuration is ever slower than brute force.

To understand \emph{why} the gap widens so sharply with scale, we fit simple cost models to SEARLE's eight measured (gallery size $N$, latency) pairs:
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{FlatIP}}(N) = a\,N + b, \qquad L_{\text{HNSWFlat}}(N) = c\,\ln N + d
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
Least-squares fitting gives $a{=}2.73{\times}10^{-5}$, $b{=}{-}0.085$ ($R^2{=}0.999$) and $c{=}0.028$, $d{=}{-}0.201$ ($R^2{=}0.885$), both in milliseconds. Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover} plots the two fitted curves against the eight observed points on log-log axes. At every \emph{measured} point, HNSWFlat is faster than FlatIP. The two \emph{fitted} curves cross near $N^*{\approx}4{,}300$, a small-sample fitting artifact; this should not be read as evidence that FlatIP is ever actually faster, since the markers, not the lines, are ground truth. The fit's value is the qualitative growth-rate argument: a linear-in-$N$ cost climbing steeply while a logarithmic-in-$N$ cost stays nearly flat, explaining why speedup widens from $\sim\!1$--$5\times$ at CIRR/FashionIQ scale to $22.8\times$ at CIRCO scale.

ANN indexing is therefore not risk-free for IVFFlat at small scale (it can genuinely be slower there), but for HNSWFlat the trade-off is better described as ``at least as fast at every gallery size we measured, with a margin that grows log-linearly with $N$'' rather than a scale threshold to cross.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{Finding 2: IVFPQ does not reach the ``retains recall'' regime tested here; it is a different, more extreme trade-off.} IVFPQ delivers the largest speedups observed (up to $149.4\times$) and the smallest memory footprint (as low as $1.3\%$ of the exact index), directly reflecting Product Quantization's compression strength, but its Recall Retention@10 across the entire sweep ranges only $49.7$--$58.7\%$ on CIRR, $23.2$--$32.1\%$ on FashionIQ, and $10.9$--$20.3\%$ on CIRCO. No $m \in \{8, 16\}$ configuration on any dataset approaches the $95\%$ bar. These figures are exact point estimates, not averages: IVFPQ was empirically confirmed deterministic across rebuilds (Section~\ref{subsec:repeated}), so repeated construction would not change them.

To understand \emph{why}, we measured the actual per-vector reconstruction error FAISS's index.reconstruct() produces after PQ encoding on SEARLE/CIRCO's worst case, and compared it against the score gap needed for correct ranking. Since $\|q\|{=}\|x\|{=}1$, Cauchy-Schwarz gives:
\begin{equation}
|q \cdot x - q \cdot \hat{x}| = |q \cdot (x - \hat{x})| \leq \|x - \hat{x}\|_2
\label{eq:pqbound}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:pqerror} reports both sides: the reconstruction error is $\approx\!440$--$470\times$ larger than the rank-10/11 score gap, large enough by Eq.~\eqref{eq:pqbound} to overturn the correct ranking for essentially every query, consistent with the observed $14.5$--$20.3\%$ retention.

The $512$--$768$-d CLIP-derived embeddings used by all three CIR models are simply too high-dimensional for $m\in\{8,16\}$ sub-quantizers ($64$--$96$ raw dimensions compressed into a single 8-bit code each) to reconstruct with the precision this task's fine-grained top-10 ranking requires. IVFPQ should therefore be presented as a distinct operating point (extreme compression, large accuracy cost, quantifiably explained by its reconstruction error dwarfing the task's discriminative score gap) rather than folded into the same ``fast with minimal loss'' conclusion drawn for IVFFlat/HNSWFlat; it is appropriate when memory, not latency, is the binding constraint.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent\textbf{Finding 3: HNSWFlat is usually, but not always, faster than IVFFlat at matched retention, showing that the ranking is model-dependent.} In most (dataset, model) pairs, HNSWFlat reaches a given retention level at a higher speedup than IVFFlat (e.g., CIRCO/SEARLE: $22.8\times$ vs.\ $6.8\times$ at $\sim\!96$--$97\%$ retention), consistent with HNSWFlat's known theoretical advantage. The exception is CIRCO/Pic2Word, where HNSWFlat is faster ($5.3\times$) but never reaches $95\%$ even averaged over 5 independent builds (best: $91.5\%\pm0.3\%$), while IVFFlat reaches $97.2\%$ at only $1.4\times$.

We tested, rather than assumed, two standard geometric explanations for this exception on the same CIRCO gallery images embedded by both models: Relative Contrast (Beyer et al.-style ratio of mean random-pair distance to nearest-neighbor distance) and Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (Levina-Bickel MLE estimator, $k{=}20$). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} shows neither differs meaningfully between SEARLE and Pic2Word (if anything, in the wrong direction to explain Pic2Word's difficulty), so global embedding-space hardness is not the mechanism.

Re-examining our own sweep data instead shows a consistent, mechanistic signal: Pic2Word's retention gain from doubling the HNSW graph degree ($M{:}16\!\to\!32$ at efSearch$=128$) is nearly double SEARLE's on CIRCO, and directionally larger on CIRR too, though both stay comfortably above $95\%$ there (Fig.~\ref{fig:msensitivity}). This is consistent with HNSW's known requirement that graph degree $M$ scale with embedding dimensionality: Pic2Word's $768$-d space is the highest-dimensional of the three models (vs.\ SEARLE's $512$-d), yet our swept grid capped $M$ at $32$ uniformly across all models. The pattern does not replicate on FashionIQ, where all three models reach $>92\%$ retention even at $M{=}16$ and gallery scale is much smaller, suggesting graph under-provisioning matters specifically at CIRCO's combination of scale and dimensionality.

This is a plausible, partially-confirmed mechanism rather than a fully generalized rule (consistent with our original caution that the HNSWFlat/IVFFlat ordering should not be assumed without per-model verification), and points to a concrete follow-up: sweep $M>32$ for Pic2Word on CIRCO to test whether retention crosses $95\%$ with adequate graph connectivity.

\textbf{End-to-End Latency vs.\ Search Speedup.} Our evaluation measures only the \emph{search} phase. The end-to-end latency also includes the \emph{encoding} phase ($\sim$15--25\,ms per query on our CPU). For small galleries (FashionIQ, CIRR), FlatIP search takes under 0.1\,ms, so even a $5\times$ speedup saves mere fractions of a millisecond. At CIRCO's scale (123K images), FlatIP grows to 3.3\,ms; HNSWFlat's $22.8\times$ speedup reduces this to 0.15\,ms, meaningful in high-QPS serving or multi-stage retrieval pipelines.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reproduced three CIR models (CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, Pic2Word) under a single evaluation harness and measured how FAISS-based ANN indexing trades retrieval quality for query cost across three benchmarks of increasing scale. Harness validation surfaced and fixed two reproducibility bugs (a normalization mismatch causing near-total self-retrieval, and a batch-indexing crash), without which any downstream ANN measurement would have been meaningless. The central finding is that the accuracy/speed trade-off is scale-dependent: HNSWFlat and IVFFlat both deliver large speedups on the 123K-image CIRCO gallery, but on the 2--6K-image galleries the margin shrinks to $1$--$5\times$. IVFPQ's extreme speed and memory gains come at an accuracy cost far outside the ``retains recall'' regime, explained by its reconstruction error dwarfing the task's rank-discrimination gap.

Two limitations bound these conclusions. First, the Pic2Word checkpoint originates from an unverified community mirror, compensated only by behavioral (not cryptographic) verification against published numbers. Second, the parameter grid (2--3 values per axis, chosen under a fixed time budget) is coarse; in particular, no tested IVFPQ configuration approached the $95\%$ retention bar, leaving open whether a wider grid (larger $m$, larger nlist) could close some of that gap. Future work should sweep HNSWFlat's graph degree beyond $M{=}32$ for Pic2Word on CIRCO to test the dimensionality-driven under-provisioning hypothesis of Finding~3, submit predictions to the official CIRCO test-set evaluation server to obtain a fully independent accuracy check, and widen the IVFPQ parameter grid to determine whether its accuracy cost can be meaningfully reduced or is inherent to compressing $512$--$768$-d embeddings at $m\in\{8,16\}$.
