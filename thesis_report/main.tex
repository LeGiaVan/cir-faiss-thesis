\begin{abstract}
Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) systems answer queries formed by a reference image and a free-text modifier. Recent state-of-the-art models -- CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, and Pic2Word -- report Recall@K and mAP@K under exhaustive, exact (brute-force) nearest-neighbor search, an assumption that breaks down once the image gallery grows beyond what a single query can scan in real time. This paper studies what happens when exact search is replaced by Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) indexing from the FAISS library (\texttt{IndexIVFFlat}, \texttt{IndexHNSWFlat}, \texttt{IndexIVFPQ}) across three CIR models and three benchmarks of increasing gallery scale (FashionIQ, 3.8K--6.3K images; CIRR, 2.3K images; CIRCO, 123K images). We first reproduce the published validation-set results, uncovering and fixing two significant reproducibility bugs -- a feature-normalization mismatch that caused 98.4\% of CIRR queries to retrieve their own reference image, and a batch-indexing bug that crashed Pic2Word query encoding entirely -- before any ANN experiment could be considered valid. We then introduce \emph{Recall Retention}, a metric that measures an ANN index's neighbor-set overlap with exact search directly, decoupling indexing error from a model's inherent retrieval quality, and sweep 120 index configurations with gallery-size-adaptive clustering parameters. The results show that the accuracy/speed trade-off is strongly scale-dependent: HNSWFlat reaches a $22.8\times$ speedup at $97.0\%\pm0.3\%$ Recall Retention@10 (mean $\pm$ std over 5 independently rebuilt indices) on the 123K-image CIRCO gallery, shrinking to sub-$5\times$ (never below $1\times$ at any of the five gallery sizes actually measured) on the 2--6K-image FashionIQ and CIRR galleries, while IVFFlat can turn genuinely counter-productive (speedup $<1\times$) at that same small scale; IVFPQ, meanwhile, trades up to $149.4\times$ speedup and a $98.7\%$ memory reduction for Recall Retention as low as $10.9\%$ (best case across the swept grid: $58.7\%$), which we trace to reconstruction error roughly two orders of magnitude larger than the score gap needed for correct top-10 ranking. All latency figures and HNSWFlat accuracy figures are averaged over repeated trials rather than single runs, to guard against measurement noise. These findings indicate that ANN indexing is not a uniformly beneficial substitute for exact search in CIR, but a scale- and index-type-dependent decision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) is the task of retrieving an image from a gallery given a query composed of a reference image and a natural-language modifier describing a desired change -- for example, a photograph of a red dress paired with the instruction ``the same but in blue with shorter sleeves.'' The task is central to e-commerce visual search, where users routinely wish to refine a search result relative to a product they have already found. Since 2023, three models have substantially advanced CIR accuracy by building on the joint image-text embedding space of CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}: CLIP4Cir~\cite{baldrati2023clip4cir}, a supervised approach that fuses image and text features through a trained Combiner network; and SEARLE~\cite{baldrati2023searle} and Pic2Word~\cite{saito2023pic2word}, two zero-shot approaches that map the reference image into a pseudo-word token consumed by the frozen CLIP text encoder.

All three papers evaluate retrieval quality by computing the cosine similarity between the query vector and \emph{every} gallery vector -- an exhaustive, $O(N)$ brute-force search. On the galleries used for evaluation (a few thousand images for FashionIQ and CIRR), this is inexpensive: our own brute-force measurements average well under a millisecond per query. On CIRCO, whose gallery already contains 123,403 images, brute-force search already costs on the order of 1--3~ms per query in our measurements (Section~\ref{sec:results}) -- and a production catalog with millions of listings would push this well past what a real-time service can afford. The standard industrial remedy is Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search, implemented in libraries such as FAISS~\cite{johnson2019faiss}, which trades a controlled amount of accuracy for sub-linear query cost. Yet none of the three CIR papers above -- nor, to our knowledge, any published CIR work -- reports what happens to retrieval quality once exact search is replaced by an ANN index. The trade-off is assumed, not measured.

This gap motivates the following research question: \emph{does replacing exact search with FAISS-based approximate nearest-neighbor indexing preserve Composed Image Retrieval quality while reducing query cost, and how does this trade-off depend on gallery scale and on the CIR model family (supervised vs.\ zero-shot)?}

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a single reproducible evaluation harness around a common \texttt{BaseAdapter} interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) that wraps CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, and Pic2Word, each evaluated under its dataset-specific protocol (per-category indexing for FashionIQ, subset masking for CIRR, multi-ground-truth mAP for CIRCO).
    \item In the course of validating this harness against the published validation-set numbers, we uncover and fix two reproducibility bugs that would otherwise have invalidated every downstream ANN experiment: a query-feature normalization mismatch that caused 98.4\% of CLIP4Cir/CIRR queries to retrieve their own reference image instead of the target, and a batch-indexing bug that crashed Pic2Word query encoding outright. We report this diagnostic process as a methodological contribution in its own right.
    \item We introduce \emph{Recall Retention}, a metric that measures the neighbor-set overlap between an ANN index and exact search directly, isolating indexing error from the model's own retrieval quality -- a distinction the raw Recall@K metrics used in the source papers cannot make.
    \item We sweep 120 FAISS index configurations across 14 (model, dataset) combinations with a gallery-size-adaptive parameter grid, and show that the resulting accuracy/speed/memory trade-off depends strongly on gallery scale and on the index family, yielding concrete deployment guidance rather than a single blanket recommendation.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork} reviews CIR models and ANN search algorithms. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the evaluation harness, the per-dataset protocol, and the Recall Retention metric. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the reproducibility validation, the sweep results, and discusses the three main findings. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\subsection{Composed Image Retrieval Models}
\label{subsec:cir-models}

CLIP4Cir~\cite{baldrati2023clip4cir} fine-tunes a CLIP backbone (ResNet-50x4) and trains a lightweight \emph{Combiner} network that fuses the reference-image and text-modifier embeddings through a learned dynamic scalar gate, producing a single query vector. It is evaluated on FashionIQ~\cite{wu2021fashioniq} and CIRR~\cite{liu2021cirr} in a fully supervised setting, i.e., trained end-to-end on each dataset's triplets.

SEARLE~\cite{baldrati2023searle} instead targets the zero-shot setting: a small network $\Phi$ maps the reference image into a pseudo-word token $S{*}$, which is spliced into the modifier text (``a photo of $S{*}$ that {\dots}'') and encoded by an otherwise frozen CLIP text encoder. Because it requires no CIR-specific training data, SEARLE generalizes to open-domain galleries, and its authors introduce CIRCO, a 123K-image benchmark built on the COCO2017 unlabeled pool, specifically to evaluate this generalization at scale.

Pic2Word~\cite{saito2023pic2word} follows the same pseudo-word-token philosophy as SEARLE but replaces the textual-inversion network with a simpler MLP (\texttt{IM2TEXT}) trained on large-scale image-caption pairs. It is evaluated on the same three benchmarks and serves in this paper as a second, architecturally independent zero-shot model against which SEARLE's behavior can be cross-checked.

\subsection{Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search}
\label{subsec:ann}

FAISS~\cite{johnson2019faiss} provides several families of ANN index relevant to this study. \texttt{IndexIVFFlat} partitions the vector space into \texttt{nlist} Voronoi cells via $k$-means and restricts search to the \texttt{nprobe} nearest cells. \texttt{IndexHNSWFlat} builds a multi-layer navigable small-world graph and performs greedy graph traversal, controlled by graph degree $M$ and search-time beam width \texttt{efSearch}. \texttt{IndexIVFPQ} combines IVF partitioning with Product Quantization, compressing each vector into $m$ sub-quantized codes to reduce memory footprint at the cost of reconstruction error. All three are approximations of \texttt{IndexFlatIP}, the exhaustive exact-search baseline used (implicitly) by the CIR papers reviewed above.

\subsection{Research Gap Summary}
\label{subsec:research-gap}

The CIR models reviewed in Section~\ref{subsec:cir-models} optimize exclusively at the \emph{model layer} -- how to produce the most accurate query vector -- and evaluate that vector with an $O(N)$ exhaustive comparison against the gallery. None examines the \emph{index layer}: how retrieval quality degrades, and by how much query cost falls, when exact search is replaced by one of the ANN structures described in Section~\ref{subsec:ann}. This is precisely the gap this paper addresses, jointly across a supervised model (CLIP4Cir) and two independently architected zero-shot models (SEARLE, Pic2Word), and across three benchmarks spanning a 50$\times$ range of gallery scale.

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{subsec:architecture}

All three CIR models, despite unrelated training procedures, reduce at inference time to the same operation: produce one $d$-dimensional, $L_2$-normalized vector per query, to be compared against $d$-dimensional gallery vectors by cosine similarity ($d=640$ for CLIP4Cir, $512$ for SEARLE, $768$ for Pic2Word). We exploit this to define a single \texttt{BaseAdapter} interface (\texttt{extract\_image\_features}, \texttt{extract\_query\_features}) implemented once per model, and a single evaluation harness (\texttt{FaissEvaluator}) that consumes the resulting vectors regardless of which model produced them. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, the pipeline is split into two phases: gallery images are embedded once and indexed (Phase 1), after which arbitrary queries are embedded and searched against that index (Phase 2). This separation lets the (expensive, one-off) embedding step be fully decoupled from the (repeated) FAISS benchmarking step.

\subsection{FAISS Index Configurations and Adaptive Parameter Sweep}
\label{subsec:sweep}

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} lists the index types and parameter grid swept for every (model, dataset) combination. A first implementation used a fixed \texttt{nlist} across all datasets; this triggered FAISS's own ``too few points per centroid'' warning on the smaller galleries (CIRR, FashionIQ) and produced artificially collapsed IVFFlat/IVFPQ recall as a result. FAISS's official guidance~\cite{johnson2019faiss,faisswiki2024guidelines} recommends $\texttt{nlist} \in [4\sqrt{N}, 16\sqrt{N}]$ together with a training-data floor of roughly $30 \times \texttt{nlist}$ vectors per index -- i.e., $N \gtrsim 30\,\texttt{nlist}$ for stable $k$-means centroids. Applying the guideline's own lower bound, $\texttt{nlist} = 4\sqrt{N}$, to our two smaller-scale benchmarks violates this floor: for CIRR ($N{=}2{,}297$) it requires $N \geq 5{,}751$ training vectors while only $2{,}297$ exist, and the shortfall is similar for every FashionIQ category ($N{=}3{,}817$--$6{,}346$, requiring $7{,}414$--$9{,}559$). Only on CIRCO ($N{=}123{,}403$) does $4\sqrt{N}=1{,}405$ comfortably clear its own floor ($42{,}155 \leq 123{,}403$). We therefore extend the swept grid one step below the official range, to $\texttt{nlist}_{\text{small}} \approx \sqrt{N}$ -- which satisfies the $30\times$ floor on every benchmark tested, including CIRR and FashionIQ -- while retaining $\texttt{nlist}_{\text{large}} \approx 4\sqrt{N}$, the official range's own lower bound, for IVFFlat; IVFPQ uses $\texttt{nlist}_{\text{small}}$ throughout, since its already-small per-cell vector budget (after Product Quantization) makes the more conservative choice necessary for stable cluster training. In short, the adaptive formula is not an arbitrary interpolation but the range of \texttt{nlist} values that keeps every one of our galleries above FAISS's own minimum-training-data guideline, which the fixed-\texttt{nlist} baseline and even the official range's lower bound fail to do at CIR-typical gallery scales.

\subsection{Recall Retention Metric}
\label{subsec:retention}

The Recall@K and mAP@K metrics used by the source papers compare retrieved results against ground truth, conflating two distinct sources of error: the model's own retrieval quality, and the approximation error introduced by the index. To isolate the latter, we define Recall Retention@K as the mean neighbor-set overlap between an ANN index's top-$K$ result and exact search's top-$K$ result for the same query, independent of whether either set contains the ground-truth target:
\begin{equation}
\text{RecallRetention@}K = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{\left| \text{top-}K_{\text{ANN}}(q) \cap \text{top-}K_{\text{Flat}}(q) \right|}{K}
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}$ is the query set and $\text{top-}K_{\text{Flat}}(q)$ is always computed first and cached as the baseline for every subsequent ANN configuration on the same (model, dataset) pair. This is the standard notion of ``recall'' in the ANN-benchmarking literature and should not be confused with Information-Retrieval-style Recall@K reported elsewhere in this paper.

\subsection{Repeated-Measurement Protocol}
\label{subsec:repeated}

A single run of any benchmark conflates the quantity of interest with measurement noise -- system jitter for latency, and, for indices with a randomized construction procedure, run-to-run variation in the index itself. Before designing the repeated-measurement protocol, we first tested empirically, rather than assumed, which of the four index types actually exhibit construction randomness: \texttt{IndexFlatIP}, \texttt{IndexIVFFlat}, and \texttt{IndexIVFPQ} were rebuilt from the same vectors ten times each and found to be bit-identical across rebuilds (FAISS's k-means training uses a fixed default seed), whereas \texttt{IndexHNSWFlat} rebuilds differed by approximately $0.5\%$ of search results, traceable to the random level assignment step in HNSW graph construction. This finding fixes the design of the protocol: \emph{latency and QPS} are measured with 1 warm-up query batch followed by $N=7$ timed search trials on a single fixed index, for every index type, since search-time system noise affects all of them equally; \emph{Recall Retention and speedup}, by contrast, are only re-measured across independent rebuilds for HNSWFlat -- 5 independent graph constructions, each searched 3 times -- since the other three index types are deterministic and a second rebuild would reproduce identical numbers. All HNSWFlat figures reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are therefore mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 5 builds; figures for FlatIP, IVFFlat, and IVFPQ are single point estimates with empirically-justified zero variance across rebuilds (search-level timing noise is still averaged over 7 trials for all four).

\subsection{Sanity-Check Verification Protocol}
\label{subsec:sanitycheck}

Before any ANN experiment is run, brute-force (\texttt{IndexFlatIP}) Recall@K/mAP@K for every (model, dataset) pair is compared against the corresponding published validation-set numbers, with a tolerance of roughly 1--2 percentage points. This step is treated as a hard gate: if the exact-search baseline itself does not match the published protocol, no conclusion drawn from an approximate index built on top of it can be trusted. Section~\ref{subsec:sanitycheckresults} reports the outcome of this gate, including two significant bugs it surfaced.

\subsection{Sanity-Check: Validation Against Published Results}
\label{subsec:sanitycheckresults}

Applying the protocol of Section~\ref{subsec:sanitycheck} initially surfaced a severe anomaly: CLIP4Cir on CIRR scored Recall@1~$=0.81\%$, far below even the weakest configuration reported in the original paper ($21.38\%$). Diagnosis proceeded by elimination: the checkpoint's SHA-256 hash matched a fresh download from the authors' Google Drive folder (ruling out a corrupted file), and a targeted script revealed that $98.4\%$ of queries retrieved \emph{the reference image itself} at rank 1 instead of the target. Inspecting the official CLIP4Cir training and validation code showed that the Combiner network is trained and evaluated with the image feature $L_2$-normalized but the text feature left \emph{unnormalized}; our adapter normalized both, distorting the learned residual gate $\texttt{output} = \text{MLP}(\cdot) + \text{gate}\cdot\text{text} + (1-\text{gate})\cdot\text{image}$ enough that the image term dominated almost every query. Removing the erroneous text normalization dropped the self-retrieval rate to $7.5\%$ and raised Recall@1 to $37.38\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:sanitycheck}), within $2.5$--$5.8$ points of the published (RN50, a smaller backbone than ours) validation numbers. A second bug -- Pic2Word's query encoder called a single-sample-only tensor-indexing routine (\texttt{encode\_text\_img\_vis}) under batched inference, crashing immediately -- was fixed by switching to the batch-safe \texttt{encode\_text\_img\_retrieval} routine used in the authors' own evaluation script.

Seven of the nine (model, dataset) cells meet or nearly meet the sanity-check tolerance; two zero-shot models (SEARLE and Pic2Word) independently matched CIRCO within $\leq\!1$ mAP point of each other's deviation from the published table, providing cross-model evidence that the evaluation harness itself -- as opposed to any single checkpoint -- is implemented correctly.

\textbf{Finding 1: HNSWFlat is the safest choice, and its speed advantage over brute force widens predictably with gallery scale.} On CIRCO (123K images), HNSWFlat reaches a $22.8\times$ speedup (SEARLE) at $97.0\%\pm0.3\%$ Recall Retention@10 (mean $\pm$ std, 5 independent graph builds; Section~\ref{subsec:repeated}) -- a favorable trade by any standard. On CIRR and FashionIQ (2.3K--6.3K images), the best achievable speedup at the same retention bar collapses to $1$--$5\times$. We initially attributed occasional sub-brute-force speedups at this scale to HNSWFlat itself, but re-examining the full 120-configuration sweep shows every configuration with speedup $<1\times$ on CIRR/FashionIQ is an \texttt{IVFFlat} configuration -- no \texttt{HNSWFlat} configuration in the entire sweep is ever slower than brute force.

To understand \emph{why} the HNSWFlat/FlatIP gap still widens so sharply with scale rather than staying near $1\times$, we fit simple cost models to SEARLE's five measured (gallery size $N$, latency) pairs (three FashionIQ categories, CIRR, CIRCO): an exhaustive linear scan for FlatIP, and logarithmic graph-traversal cost for HNSWFlat --
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{FlatIP}}(N) = a\,N + b, \qquad L_{\text{HNSWFlat}}(N) = c\,\ln N + d
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
Least-squares fitting gives $a=2.8\times10^{-5}$, $b=-0.076$ ($R^2=0.9996$) and $c=0.032$, $d=-0.232$ ($R^2=0.990$), both in milliseconds. Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover} plots the two fitted curves against the five observed points on log-log axes. At every one of the five \emph{actually measured} points, HNSWFlat's raw latency is lower than FlatIP's -- including at $N{=}3{,}817$, the closest pair (0.036~ms vs.\ 0.041~ms). The two \emph{fitted} curves themselves, however, cross near $N^*\approx3{,}870$: below this point the fitted FlatIP line dips under the fitted HNSWFlat curve, an artifact of forcing a single two-parameter line through a tight cluster of four small-$N$ points and one much larger, high-leverage point at $N{=}123{,}403$. We report this honestly rather than paper over it -- with only five points, a linear fit dominated by one far outlier is not a reliable interpolation device near the cluster's edge, and the fitted crossover should not be read as evidence that FlatIP is ever actually faster in this regime; the markers, not the lines, are the ground truth. What the fit \emph{is} reliable for is the qualitative growth-rate argument it was built to support: a linear-in-$N$ cost climbing steeply while a logarithmic-in-$N$ cost stays nearly flat, which is why the speedup margin widens from $\sim\!1$--$5\times$ at CIRR/FashionIQ scale to $22.8\times$ at CIRCO scale.

ANN indexing is therefore not risk-free for IVFFlat at small scale (it can genuinely be slower there), but for HNSWFlat the trade-off is better described as ``at least as fast at every gallery size we measured, with a margin that grows log-linearly with $N$'' rather than a scale threshold to cross.

\textbf{Finding 2: IVFPQ does not reach the ``retains recall'' regime tested here -- it is a different, more extreme trade-off.} IVFPQ delivers the largest speedups observed (up to $149.4\times$) and the smallest memory footprint (as low as $1.3\%$ of the exact index), directly reflecting Product Quantization's compression strength, but its Recall Retention@10 across the entire sweep ranges only $49.7$--$58.7\%$ on CIRR, $23.2$--$32.1\%$ on FashionIQ, and $10.9$--$20.3\%$ on CIRCO -- no $m \in \{8, 16\}$ configuration on any dataset approaches the $95\%$ bar. These figures are exact point estimates, not averages: IVFPQ was empirically confirmed deterministic across rebuilds (Section~\ref{subsec:repeated}), so repeated construction would not change them.

To understand \emph{why} the loss is this severe rather than simply report it, we measured, on SEARLE/CIRCO's worst-case configuration, the actual per-vector reconstruction error FAISS's own \texttt{index.reconstruct()} produces after PQ encoding, and compared it against the score gap a query actually needs to rank correctly. Because both query $q$ and gallery vector $x$ are $L_2$-normalized ($\|q\|=\|x\|=1$), Cauchy-Schwarz gives a rigorous, query-independent upper bound on how much PQ quantization alone can perturb a similarity score:
\begin{equation}
\left| q\cdot x - q\cdot\hat{x} \right| = \left| q\cdot(x-\hat{x}) \right| \;\leq\; \|x-\hat{x}\|_2
\label{eq:cauchyschwarz}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:pqerror} reports both sides of this bound. The reconstruction error is $\approx\!440$--$470\times$ larger than the score gap needed to keep the true rank-10 candidate ahead of rank-11 -- large enough, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cauchyschwarz}, to overturn the correct ranking for essentially every query, consistent with the observed $14.5$--$20.3\%$ retention on this exact configuration (directly re-measured, matching the swept values).

The $512$--$768$-d CLIP-derived embeddings used by all three CIR models are simply too high-dimensional for $m\in\{8,16\}$ sub-quantizers ($64$--$96$ raw dimensions compressed into a single 8-bit code each) to reconstruct with the precision this task's fine-grained top-10 ranking requires. IVFPQ should therefore be presented as a distinct operating point (extreme compression, large accuracy cost, quantifiably explained by its reconstruction error dwarfing the task's discriminative score gap) rather than folded into the same ``fast with minimal loss'' conclusion drawn for IVFFlat/HNSWFlat; it is appropriate when memory, not latency, is the binding constraint.

\textbf{Finding 3: HNSWFlat is usually, but not always, faster than IVFFlat at matched retention -- the ranking is model-dependent.} In most (dataset, model) pairs, HNSWFlat reaches a given retention level at a higher speedup than IVFFlat (e.g., CIRCO/SEARLE: $22.8\times$ vs.\ $6.8\times$ at $\sim\!96$--$97\%$ retention), consistent with HNSWFlat's known theoretical advantage. The exception is CIRCO/Pic2Word, where HNSWFlat is faster ($5.3\times$) but never reaches $95\%$ even averaged over 5 independent builds (best: $91.5\%\pm0.3\%$), while IVFFlat reaches $97.2\%$ at only $1.4\times$.

We tested, rather than assumed, two standard geometric explanations for this exception on the same CIRCO gallery images embedded by both models: Relative Contrast (Beyer et al.-style ratio of mean random-pair distance to nearest-neighbor distance) and Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (Levina-Bickel MLE estimator, $k{=}20$). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} shows neither differs meaningfully between SEARLE and Pic2Word -- if anything, in the wrong direction to explain Pic2Word's difficulty -- so global embedding-space hardness is not the mechanism.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reproduced three Composed Image Retrieval models (CLIP4Cir, SEARLE, Pic2Word) under a single evaluation harness and measured, for the first time to our knowledge, how FAISS-based approximate nearest-neighbor indexing trades retrieval quality for query cost on three benchmarks of increasing scale. Validating the harness against published results surfaced and fixed two reproducibility bugs -- a normalization mismatch causing near-total self-retrieval on CIRR, and a batch-indexing crash in Pic2Word -- without which any downstream ANN measurement would have been meaningless; we report this diagnostic trail as a methodological contribution alongside the benchmark itself. The central empirical finding is that the FAISS accuracy/speed trade-off is not a fixed property of an index type but a function of gallery scale: HNSWFlat and IVFFlat both deliver large, low-cost speedups on the 123K-image CIRCO gallery, but on the 2--6K-image FashionIQ/CIRR galleries only IVFFlat can turn negative (speedup $<1\times$), while HNSWFlat's margin over brute force shrinks to $1$--$5\times$ without ever reversing at any gallery size actually measured -- a margin that widens with $N$ at a rate consistent with linear-vs-logarithmic latency growth, rather than crossing an assumed scale threshold. IVFPQ's extreme speed and memory gains, in turn, come at an accuracy cost far outside the ``retains recall'' regime achieved by the other two index families, quantifiably explained by its reconstruction error dwarfing the task's rank-discrimination gap at the parameter settings tested.

Three limitations bound these conclusions. First, the CLIP4Cir/FashionIQ gap ($\approx\!11$ points) remains unresolved after ruling out checkpoint corruption, a code-level bug, caption formatting, and image resolution; we attribute it, provisionally, to a limitation of the publicly released checkpoint itself rather than to our pipeline. Second, the Pic2Word checkpoint originates from an unverified community mirror, compensated only by behavioral (not cryptographic) verification against published numbers. Third, the parameter grid (2--3 values per axis, chosen under a fixed time budget) is coarse; in particular, no tested IVFPQ configuration approached the $95\%$ retention bar, leaving open whether a wider grid (larger $m$, larger \texttt{nlist}) could close some of that gap. Future work should extend the CIRCO gallery with intermediate-scale distractor sets to test whether the fitted linear (FlatIP) and logarithmic (HNSWFlat) latency-growth models of Finding~1 hold between the 6K and 123K scales already measured, sweep HNSWFlat's graph degree beyond $M{=}32$ for Pic2Word on CIRCO to test the dimensionality-driven under-provisioning hypothesis of Finding~3, submit predictions to the official CIRCO test-set evaluation server to obtain a fully independent accuracy check, and widen the IVFPQ parameter grid to determine whether its accuracy cost can be meaningfully reduced or is inherent to compressing $512$--$768$-d embeddings at $m\in\{8,16\}$.
